# UNFURL — Compute for the absolute underdogs

> There needs to be an initiative where the big AI providers donate some compute time to a solopreneur or small unknown group in case they have something worth developing. Reading the latest back and forth between Musk and whoever it was from Anthropic about donating shares to (targeted, isolated, 0 risk) entities. If they want to help, let's see what the absolute underdogs have to offer and expose it publicly, then throw 10241024 gigatons of compute at it for a day, see what happens.

But your proposal introduces something neither approach directly addresses: **give unknown people the productive power of AI, rather than just a share of the wealth it generates.**

And importantly, select them for the potential of what they're developing, rather than their ability to attract investors, assemble a company, or market themselves.

A lone inventor might have spent ten years developing something nobody has recognized yet. Give that person access to serious computational resources, capable AI agents, and a concentrated development window. Publicly demonstrate what happens.

The experiment isn't simply whether AI can accelerate a startup. It's **what becomes possible when exceptional ideas are no longer constrained by the resources or social position of their creators.**

> the people select people... not the donors

**The public selects the people and projects worth backing.** The AI providers donate the compute and make it available to those selected.

The donors supply the resources. The people decide where those resources go.

> Though, how informed and motivated are they to vote correctly, to grasp the potential of projects, no matter how well presented, focus is a problem. Perhaps both the public and the owners of AI can put in their 5 cents or whatever the fkn term is.

Both should have a say, but neither should have exclusive control.

The public can recognize value that experts overlook. Experts can recognize potential that the public cannot easily understand. And AI itself can help people grasp the substance of unfamiliar projects before judging them.

Perhaps the crucial part is **separating evaluation from selection**.

Let the public, independent experts and AI providers examine projects from different perspectives, with AI making the technical substance understandable without reducing or distorting it.

Then let those assessments inform a selection process that gives unconventional ideas a genuine chance, including those that attract little attention.

Because popularity, expertise and commercial potential are three different things. None reliably measures how much an idea might contribute to humanity.

And perhaps **reserve some compute for projects that nobody can agree on**. Those could be the most interesting experiments of all.

> Yes, the more talk about it, the better. And this shouldn't be a single winner every year or whatever. As long as there is stuff to unfold, unfurl it.

Exactly. **An ongoing discovery process, not a competition with an arbitrary number of winners.**

The more people discuss a project, question it, understand it, challenge its assumptions and discover connections to other work, the more potential can become visible.

And selection shouldn't be the end of evaluation. Give a promising project some compute, see what unfolds, publish the findings, and let that inform what happens next. Some projects will reveal entirely new possibilities that deserve further resources.

Others might unlock something valuable for a completely different project.

**Compute follows unfolding potential, not a calendar, a fixed prize pool or a popularity ranking.**

And the public gets to witness the development of ideas, not merely vote on promises.
